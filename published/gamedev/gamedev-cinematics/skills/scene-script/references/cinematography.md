# Cinematography checks A-L

Use these as review questions. State a deliberate exception and verify its effect; stylistic choices
are not defects merely because they depart from a default.

## A. Dramatic structure

- Identify who wants what, what blocks it, what failure costs, and why the moment occurs now.
- Make the obstacle perceptible through action, image, or sound.
- Express the change as a value pair: safety -> exposure, doubt -> certainty, power -> vulnerability.
- Locate the observable action or discovery that causes the turn.
- Link beats with cause and consequence. Merge repetitions that add no new pressure or information.
- Check whether player action should trigger the threat's escalation; passive activation may weaken agency.
- Enter after routine setup and leave after the consequence registers.
- Define five planning anchors: emotion, recurring motif, physical object, disruption, final image.
- Choose an arc: pursuit, discovery, escalation/reversal, concentrated peak, or unresolved hook.
- Remove explanatory lines and check what the images still communicate. Dialogue may carry essential
  information, but record that dependency rather than claiming the wordless test passes.

## B. Shot purpose and coverage

- Give each shot one primary job and a clear function. Split competing jobs only when readability needs it.
- Include an environmental condition, physical micro-action, and recurring visual or sound detail where useful.
- Establish actor, threat, and route early; re-establish after large movement or spatially confusing coverage.
- Make the essential action face the lens and occupy enough screen area to read.
- Use tighter framing when information becomes more important, and wider framing for consequence or isolation.
- Mark indispensable shots. The causal story should survive when decorative coverage is removed.
- For a physical turning action, check effort -> failure point -> consequence. Check this: a single medium
  view may hide the exact failure point, so try a detail insert before increasing spectacle.
- Reserve any time stretch or overlapping action for an intentional emphasis; do not multiply peaks by habit.
- Treat shot-count budgets as previs estimates, then test whether each image has time to register.

## C. Lens, angle, and focus

- Record filmback with focal length; the same focal length can frame differently on different sensors.
- For a perspective camera, vertical FOV = 2 * atan(sensor height / (2 * focal length)).
  Convert radians to degrees if the engine expects degrees.
- For a 24 mm sensor height, 24/35/50/85 mm give approximately 53/38/27/16 degrees vertically.
  These examples are not an engine's default sensor contract.
- Camera distance changes perspective; focal length changes angle of view. Both affect framing.
  Measure subject bounds instead of assuming body size.
- Choose a coherent lens vocabulary. Record why a major lens change serves the scene.
- Use angle and height to express relationships, while checking that required actions remain visible.
- Name the focus subject independently of the look target; use deep focus when multiple planes carry meaning.
- Motivate focus pulls with an entrance, look, sound, or information change.

## D. Composition and intensity

- Choose one primary focal point and track it at thumbnail size.
- Check headroom, look room, and movement lead room; constriction can deliberately imply pressure.
- In a wide shot, use foreground framing, midground action, and background context when the space supports it.
- Keep foreground dressing clear of essential action and account for moving occluders.
- Use framing, negative space, scale, and value to direct attention.
- Dress what the lens sees, including behind actors after movement; hide set boundaries credibly.
- Graph intensity through shot scale, contrast, movement, cut rate, and sound.
  Plan a principal peak and a quieter interval that gives it contrast.

## E. Spatial continuity

- Draw the action axis between the current subjects of attention. Record each camera's side.
- Cross the axis with a visible move, a subject crossing, a neutral view, or clear re-establishment.
- Preserve travel direction and eyelines unless disorientation is intentional and recoverable.
- For a match on action, keep movement phase, direction, speed, and prop state consistent.
- Check adjacent views of the same subject for accidental jump cuts. A substantial angle change
  (roughly 30 degrees) or two shot-size steps is a useful diagnostic, not an automatic guarantee.
- Place the next focal point near the outgoing eye position when fast comprehension matters.

## F. Camera movement

- State a motive: follow, reveal, redirect attention, or register a relationship change. Otherwise try a hold.
- Give a shot one dominant movement and enough time to settle after a reveal.
- Use a push, retreat, track, rise, or handheld treatment for a named effect rather than decoration.
- Check acceleration and deceleration; a cut must not accidentally become a long blend.
- Avoid combining aggressive movement and rapid cutting when the action becomes hard to parse.
- Use a cut for a new viewpoint or moment; use movement when following attention within the moment.
- Use black or a dissolve to express the intended transition, not to conceal unresolved geography.

## G. Editing and rhythm

- Protect emotional meaning, causal information, and rhythm before preserving minor continuity niceties.
- Cut after the image's idea registers; an unfinished look or reaction can erase its meaning.
- Previs starting ranges: calm context 4-8 s; exchange 2.5-5 s; rising tension 1.5-3 s;
  brief peak images around 0.7-1.5 s. Test exceptions and actual reading demands.
- Check for a pause or reduction before impact and a held consequence afterward.
- A wide with several facts usually needs more reading time than a simple detail.
- Hold reactions when the beat concerns meaning rather than mechanics.
- Use cut-on-action, match, smash, or overlapping sound deliberately. Distinguish a tonal jolt from a mistake.
- Read the whole passage aloud and in a timed animatic; individual cards cannot prove rhythm.

## H. Lighting

- Identify a credible source for each dominant light, including off-screen sources established by context.
- Separate the subject through value, hue, edge light, focus, or background choice; check in greyscale.
- Assign value groups and contrast according to the intended mood and display conditions.
- Keep meaningful shadow detail where it carries information. Pure black can be intentional.
- Maintain a consistent environmental condition across shots of the same moment.
- Change lighting emphasis when the drama warrants it; do not force the hardest contrast onto an unreadable turn.

## I. Blocking and performance

- Block intentions first, then find views that show them.
- For each actor, name what they approach, avoid, watch, touch, and change.
- Stage power through scale, height, depth, distance, light, and access to the route.
- Express emotion through features the body actually has: posture, pace, orientation, gesture, gaze, or breath.
- Define anticipation, the key pose, and the hold. Check the pose as a silhouette at delivery scale.
- Let a look or reaction motivate a cut. Check this: a listener's eyeline can miss a much smaller actor;
  verify the actual target height rather than copying a default look-at offset.
- Place entrances and crossings on meaningful beats, and retain body and prop continuity.

## J. Sound

- Name ambience, foley, voice, accents, and intended silence for each shot.
- Silence is an authored sound state; distinguish it from a missing audio pass.
- Try a brief reduction before a key impact; tune its length to pace rather than applying a fixed delay.
- A sound arriving before its image can prepare a reveal; a sound carried over a cut can hold a reaction.
- Avoid dialogue masking a small sound on which the causal story depends.
- Check the mix in context, including music, spatial distance, and subtitle-only comprehension.

## K. Dialogue and reading

- Schedule speech and internal text together; avoid competing reading tasks across UI regions.
- Give each line a speaker, channel, cue, end, and image relationship.
- Start around 15-17 characters/s over action, then calibrate for language, font, complexity, and audience.
  Around 40-42 characters per line and at least 1 s are planning checks, not universal accessibility limits.
- Keep critical visual actions free of long reading demands. Shorten, move, or extend a line as needed.
- Write within each speaker's knowledge and voice; remove captions that merely repeat the image.
- Test localized text and user text-size settings, not only the writing language.

## L. Gameplay around the scene

- Check whether playable staging could preserve agency and deliver the same purpose.
- Continue the player's incoming action or motivate the interruption.
- Return control with a clear goal, compatible screen direction, readable threat, and fair response window.
- Match the handoff distance to the next mechanic. Check this: an opening melee tell may become irrelevant
  if control returns so far away that the player must traverse an empty approach.
- Define skip, retry, pause, interruption, and cancellation outcomes. Skip must preserve required gameplay state.
- Avoid repeating nonessential scenes on failure when the player already understands them.
- Check that staged abilities agree with what play and fiction permit.
- In variable layouts, use meaningful anchors and validate visibility and clearances at runtime.
